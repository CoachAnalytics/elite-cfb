Home and Away

Every measure split by where the game was played, and how that compares with what happens to everybody else.

The small number beside a figure is where it ranks among the Power 4 teams, taken inside that same column — so a home number is ranked against everybody else's home numbers, and switching the opponent filter re-ranks against the same comparable set. A rank built on two games is as thin as the number under it; the warning above the table says when that is the case.

Reading it. Home − away is this team's gap. League is the same gap for everybody, and vs league is the difference between the two — which is the column that matters. A team whose offense is better at home is not interesting; nearly every offense is. A team whose gap is twice the league's, or the wrong way round, is.

Why the opponent filter is there. A college schedule is not balanced: roughly three in five home games are against another Power 4 side, against almost all of the away ones. Teams host the opponents they expect to beat and travel to play the ones they do not. Switch the filter and watch the League column shrink — across 2023 to 2026 the league-wide home edge falls from 0.085 EPA per play to 0.027 once the two columns are made comparable. Two thirds of what looks like home-field advantage is the schedule. Use the filter before drawing a conclusion about a team, and read the unfiltered view as a description of what happened rather than of what the team is.

Neutral sites appear where a team has played one, and a season gives most teams none or one, so read that column as an anecdote. League-wide they behave like road games rather than something in between: away and neutral land within a thousandth of each other.

Garbage time is left out of the rate measures. Points, penalties and turnovers per game are the whole game, as the scoreboard had them.
